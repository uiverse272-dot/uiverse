The logbook habit

Every ship I have worked on kept a logbook, and not one of them kept it for the sailors on watch. It was written for whoever came aboard next, tired and short of context, at three in the morning.

Write for the next watch

Most working documents fail the same way: they record what was decided and leave out what was known at the time. Six months later the decision looks strange, and nobody can say whether it still holds. A good entry is plainer than you think it needs to be: the hour, the conditions, what you did, and what you expected to happen next.

Kofi Mensah, who taught me to keep one on the Tórshavn ferry, had a rule. If a stranger couldn’t take over your watch from the page, you hadn’t finished writing.

Write the entry you will wish you had found.

What goes in an entry

Four lines are enough, and the discipline is in keeping to them.

  • The time, and who is writing.
  • What you can see from where you stand.
  • What you changed, and why you changed it.
  • What you are watching for on the next check.
Leave out your opinion of the weather. Write down the barometer.

A template worth stealing

16:40  Y. Tanaka on watch
Seen:  swell from NW, 2 m, easing
Did:   reduced to 8 knots past the bar
Next:  recheck depth at the red buoy

Swap the swell for a deploy, the bar for a release window, and it works just as well on land. The form matters less than the fact that it never changes, because a reader who knows the shape can find what they need half asleep.

Starting this week

Keep the first experiment small enough that nobody has to approve it.

  • Pick one project that changes hands often
  • Agree on the four lines with the team
  • Write an entry at every handover for two weeks
  • Read them back on a Friday and cut what nobody used

What changes

The first thing you notice is that handovers get shorter. Nobody needs the story told twice when the page already has it, and the questions people do ask are better ones.

The second takes longer. Around the third month, someone goes looking for why a thing was done and finds the answer in a stranger’s handwriting, with the hour beside it. Lucía Ferreira, our chief engineer, called that the only compliment a log ever receives.
